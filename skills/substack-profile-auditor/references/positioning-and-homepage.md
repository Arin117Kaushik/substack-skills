# Positioning, Profile, About Page, Homepage, Paid Tier

> Ideas adapted from the public Substack Starter Kit by Write Build Scale, rewritten in our own words. Changes: nothing is filled in for the user, and perks are only listed if the user will deliver them.

A new reader decides within about a minute whether to subscribe. Everything below exists to make three answers obvious: what you write about, who it is for, and what they get.

## 1. Positioning (do this first)

Three decisions, each from the user, never guessed:

- **Topics:** one to three core topics, found where the audience's problems overlap with what the user enjoys writing about.
- **Audience:** one specific group. Naming a group attracts the right people, and the wrong ones weren't going to subscribe anyway.
- **Outcome:** the problem the reader gets help with, the goal they get closer to, or the change in them.

Combine into one statement and keep it identical everywhere:

`I write about [topics] for [audience] so they can [outcome].`

Reuse it in the bio, the About page, the welcome email and the subscribe page.

If the user hasn't decided one of the three, slot it and ask. Offer `substack-interviewer` for the audience and outcome questions.

## 2. Profile bio

Answer most of these four questions in a line or two: what do you do, what is the newsletter about, who is it for, what value do they get.

Weak: "I write about interesting stuff I find. Subscribe to learn more!" (nothing a stranger can use.)
Strong shape: `[What I do] for [audience]. Subscribe for [topic one], [topic two] and [outcome].`

Profile photo: a clear, well lit photo of the user's face on a plain background, the same across platforms. People trust a person more than a logo or generated image. Say this as a suggestion, never as a Substack rule.

## 3. Publication description

Answers what it is about, who it is for, and what the reader gets, in that order. "My thoughts on life and everything in between" answers none.

## 4. About page structure

The most common mistake is a page that is entirely about the writer. Keep the story, and add the reader's side. Order:

1. Welcome line and the positioning statement
2. Three concrete benefits for the audience
3. Subscribe button
4. One link to the post that best shows the promise
5. Who the writer is: background, one photo, one memorable fact (user's own facts only)
6. What a subscriber gets and how often (frequency the user confirms)
7. Paid perks, if there is a paid tier, one line each
8. Three to six best posts
9. A closing line with the paid button, if there is one

## 5. Homepage

- **Best work first.** Lead with a pinned post and the most popular posts, not just the newest. Popular posts have already proven they land.
- **Pinned post:** one of: the best performer, an introduction to the newsletter, or the post that leads to a freebie. Re-pick it every few months.
- **Thumbnails:** a consistent custom design beats stock photos. A reusable template makes each one a few minutes of work.
- **Sections or tags:** group posts by topic so a new reader finds their interest. Match them to the core topics from step 1.

Option names in Substack's dashboard change, so tell the user what to look for rather than quoting menu paths.

## 6. Paid tier offer

Readers pay for access and outcomes, not for more posts. Menu of perks to choose from (the user picks only what they will really deliver, and each one gets a delivery frequency):

| Perk | Shape |
|---|---|
| Short PDF guide | One problem, easy to skim, reusable |
| Monthly live session | Q&A or workshop, with a replay for paid members who missed it |
| Templates and tools | Spreadsheets, checklists, proposal text, planners |
| Workshop or mini-course | One problem, 30 to 90 minutes |

Never put a perk on the About page that the user can't confirm is live. A paid perk in the draft is a slot: `[paid perk, if you offer one]`.
